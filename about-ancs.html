What an ANC commissioner does

An Advisory Neighborhood Commissioner is DC's most local elected official: an unpaid, nonpartisan neighbor elected to represent about 2,000 residents, who sits on a commission that formally advises the city on things that affect the neighborhood.

The short version

  1. ANCs advise; they don't decide. They can't veto a liquor license, a building, or a bike lane.
  2. But their advice carries legal weight that ordinary residents' advice doesn't. The city must notify the ANC in advance of many actions. When the full commission responds in writing with its reasons, the agency that decides must address each concern in writing. This is called "great weight."
  3. A commission acts as a body. Official positions come from votes at public meetings. One commissioner can't speak for the whole ANC on their own.

How ANCs are organized

ANCs were created by the 1973 Home Rule Act and first elected in 1976, so 2026 is their 50th year. DC has 46 commissions made up of 345 single member districts (SMDs), each with about 2,000 residents and one commissioner. Commissioners serve two-year terms, are elected in November of even years, and take office on January 2.

The names work like an address. "ANC 8E" is a commission in Ward 8. "SMD 8E05" is the fifth seat on that commission.

What a commissioner actually does

  • Reviews notices from the city: building and demolition permits, public space permits, zoning cases, liquor and cannabis licenses, traffic changes, and decisions about city property.
  • Takes part in public meetings: the commission must meet at least nine times a year and must set aside time at each meeting for residents to speak.
  • Negotiates with applicants: businesses seeking liquor licenses and developers seeking zoning approval usually come to the ANC first. This is where agreements on hours, noise, trash, and community benefits get worked out.
  • Takes neighbors' problems to the right agency: commissioners can't fix a missed trash pickup or a broken streetlight themselves, but they know which city staff to push.
  • Takes positions on broader issues: commissions can pass resolutions and testify to the DC Council. These carry no formal weight but show organized neighborhood opinion.

The formal powers

Advance notice

DC law requires city agencies to notify affected ANCs, usually 30 business days ahead, before many actions: zoning changes, licenses, permits, public improvements, changes in city services, and decisions about city property. The liquor and cannabis board gives 45 days' notice of license applications and renewals.

Great weight

When the full commission responds to a notice in writing and explains why, the agency's written decision must name the ANC, address each of its concerns, and explain why it does or doesn't find the ANC's advice persuasive.

Great weight is not a veto. Agencies can disagree. And it applies only when the ANC gives reasons. A letter that just says "we oppose" doesn't qualify.

A seat at the table in liquor and zoning cases

In liquor license and zoning cases, the ANC takes part as a formal party. It can protest a license, present evidence, and negotiate a written settlement agreement with the business. In large zoning cases, ANCs often negotiate community benefits such as local hiring or neighborhood investments.

What ANCs can't do

  • Approve or deny a permit, license, zoning case, or traffic change
  • Pass laws or rules, even ones that would apply only to their own area
  • Order a city agency to fix something
  • Sue the city with ANC money
  • Act through one commissioner without a vote of the commission
  • Vote in private. Any vote taken in a closed meeting is void.
  • Use ANC money, email, or office space for or against any candidate

The money

All 46 ANCs together received about $916,000 in fiscal 2025, divided by population. Commissioners are not paid, but they do oversee a budget of nearly $20,000 per year. Commissions can spend on office costs, staff, legal representation before city boards, and small grants to community groups, provided they document and report on these expenses.

Grants must serve a public purpose in the ANC area, go to an organization (not an individual or a public school), fund a specific future project, and not duplicate a service the city already provides. Every expense must be approved by a vote in a public meeting, and commissions must file quarterly financial reports or lose their funding.

ANC 8E recently lost much of its budget (and the opportunities to invest in our community that it affords) because commissioners failed to properly report spending. Not cool. Download the official auditor's report here.

Common misconceptions

What people often thinkWhat's actually true
"The ANC can block that bar, building, or bike lane."It doesn't get a veto; beyond advocacy, the ANC's leverage is notice, great weight, formal party status, and negotiation.
"The city has to do what the ANC says."The city only has to answer each ANC concern in writing and explain its decision, which may not change the outcome but can help us hold the city accountable to our community.
"ANCs are just for show."Their powers are limited, but they can drive effective city advocacy, concretely influence local development, and even fund local projects of their own.
"My commissioner said it, so that's the ANC's position."Individual commissioners can be influential local leaders, but the ANC as a whole can only adopt an official position with a majority vote at a public meeting.
"My commissioner should fix my trash pickup."Commissioners can escalate problems to the agencies that fix them, but they don't have city staff of their own like Council members.
"The ANC is basically an HOA."ANCs are not private associations, and they have no power to pass or enforce laws; their role is primarily advisory, and they are also publicly elected, publicly funded, and bound by ethics and open-meeting laws.

What the job takes

Commissioners who have served in ANCs across the city describe a steep learning curve that's mostly about how city government works, including which agencies handle what and which staff to contact.

The time commitment is substantial, with evening meetings, agency deadlines, and neighbors' requests taking up real time. Many say the payoff is meaningful and concrete, though: a crosswalk, a stop sign, a problem that finally got fixed, and getting to know more of their neighbors.

The people who do the job best tend to be reliable, responsive, and willing to listen to neighbors who disagree with them.
